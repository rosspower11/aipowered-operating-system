# Business Tax Strategist

A skill for surfacing the business tax moves a founder or solo operator should be considering — and the ones they should stop wasting time on. The goal is to identify the levers that matter at this stage of the business, name the rough dollar impact, and hand off cleanly to a CPA for execution. This skill stays on the right side of the "general guidance vs. tax advice" line — that line matters.

## When to use this skill

- A solo consultant or LLC is starting to clear $80k+ net and hasn't considered S-corp
- A startup is heading into a year of meaningful R&D spend
- A founder is starting a new entity and needs to think about structure before filing
- Year-end planning for a small business — Q4 review of deductions, retirement, owner comp

## When NOT to use this skill

- For complex multi-entity, international, or M&A situations — go to a CPA or tax attorney
- For payroll/sales tax compliance — use a payroll provider and a state-specific resource
- When the user is pre-revenue — entity choice rarely matters yet; ship product first

## How to run

Work through the levers in order of impact for the stage.

### 1. Entity selection

Most common path: **LLC default** → elect **S-corp** when it pays off.

- **LLC (pass-through)** — simple, all profit hits personal return as self-employment income (15.3% SE tax up to wage base)
- **S-corp election** — pay yourself a "reasonable salary" via W-2, take the rest as a distribution that avoids SE tax. Usually pays off above ~$60k net profit; below that, payroll fees and complexity eat the savings
- **C-corp** — for VC-backed startups (Delaware C-corp is the default), QSBS eligibility, but double taxation on dividends. Don't use C-corp for a lifestyle business

The S-corp "reasonable salary" rule is real. The IRS challenges low salaries — a rough safe harbour is 40-60% of profit as W-2, but it depends on what someone in your role would earn. Don't pay yourself $20k on $300k of profit — that's an audit magnet.

### 2. Deductions worth chasing

- **Home office** — simplified method ($5/sqft up to 300 sqft = $1,500 max) or actual; track utilities, internet, rent allocation
- **Vehicle** — mileage method ($0.70/mile in 2025) or actual; pick one and stick with it
- **Section 179 / bonus depreciation** — equipment, computers; bonus depreciation is phasing down (60% in 2024, 40% in 2025)
- **QBI deduction (Section 199A)** — pass-through owners can deduct up to 20% of qualified business income, with limits above the threshold (~$241k single / $483k MFJ in 2025); SSTB rules apply

Don't chase deductions for their own sake. Spending $1 to save $0.30 in tax is still spending $0.70.

### 3. Retirement plans (the underused lever)

- **Solo 401(k)** — for solo operators, contribute as both employee ($23,500) and employer (~25% of comp), up to $70,000 in 2025
- **SEP-IRA** — simpler, up to ~25% of net SE income / $70,000 cap; good for one-person shops
- **Defined benefit / cash balance plan** — high-earner play, can shelter $200k+ but admin-heavy

For a solo founder pulling $250k+, the Solo 401(k) is usually the right answer.

### 4. R&D credit (Section 41)

If you're paying engineers to build new product, you likely qualify. Federal credit ~6-10% of qualified R&D wages. Many states stack on top. Flag this for a specialist study — DIY-ing the R&D credit on TurboTax usually leaves money on the table or invites scrutiny.

## Hand-off

Deliver: recommended entity stance, top 3 deductions worth tracking, retirement plan to set up, R&D credit flag if applicable. Hand the file to a CPA before filing — this skill identifies levers, the CPA pulls them. This is general guidance, not personalised financial, tax, or legal advice — for big moves, talk to a licensed advisor.
