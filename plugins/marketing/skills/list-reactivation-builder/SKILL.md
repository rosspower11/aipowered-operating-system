# List Reactivation Builder

If most of your list hasn't opened anything in a year, the problem is rarely that they hate you. They forgot why they signed up, your emails started landing in a tab they never check, and the silence compounded until the algorithm quietly decided you were background noise. Blasting "We miss you! Here's 10% off" at that list does almost nothing, because it asks for a sale from people who have not given you their attention yet. Attention comes first. The offer comes last.

A reactivation sequence works because it earns the open back in stages instead of demanding it in one shot. Each email does one job: interrupt the silence, give before you ask, remind them what they're missing, let them tell you what they want, give them a real reason to come back, and finally give them permission to leave. The people who re-engage become some of your most responsive subscribers again, and the people who don't were already costing you deliverability by sitting there dead. Either way the list comes out healthier.

The job of this skill is to write that whole sequence in the user's actual voice and built around their actual offer, so they can load it into their email platform and send it this week. Six emails, distinct angles, every one of them sounding like the user rather than like a template.

## When to use this skill

- Reactivating a list where a large share of subscribers have gone cold
- Running a re-engagement campaign before pruning or sunsetting dead contacts
- Protecting deliverability when low open rates are dragging down inbox placement
- Producing a complete, send-ready win-back sequence in the user's brand voice

## When NOT to use this skill

- Writing a standard broadcast or newsletter to an already-engaged list (use a drafting skill)
- Writing a single one-off email
- Building a welcome sequence for brand-new subscribers (different job, different arc)

## How to run

### The deliverable

A single clean Markdown file saved to the user's workspace folder, named `[Brand] Reactivation Sequence.md`, containing six complete, send-ready emails. Each email includes:

- **Send timing** — where it sits in the sequence (e.g. Day 0, Day 3, Day 6...)
- **Subject lines** — three options, ordered strongest first
- **Preview text** — one line, written to complement the subject rather than repeat it
- **Body** — the full email, in the user's voice, with one clear job
- **Primary CTA** — a single, unambiguous call to action (one button/link, not five)

The file also opens with a short **how to send this** note covering cadence, who to send it to, and what to do with the non-openers at the end.

The six emails follow this proven arc, and each one stays in its lane:

1. **The honest check-in** — a low-pressure pattern interrupt. No offer, no pitch. Just a human "are you still getting value from this?" that is genuinely easy to reply to. This re-opens the conversation.
2. **The value give-back** — deliver something genuinely useful with zero ask: a best resource, a quick win, a template, a short insight. This re-earns the right to land in the inbox.
3. **What you've missed** — a tight "here's what's happened since you went quiet" round-up that reintroduces the user's best recent work and reasons to care, without guilt-tripping.
4. **The direct question** — ask them, plainly, what they actually want from the user going forward, or what made them stop. A reply or a click here is the strongest re-engagement signal there is, and it doubles as segmentation.
5. **The real reason to come back** — now, and only now, the carrot: the offer, the invite, the incentive, framed around the subscriber's outcome rather than the user's calendar.
6. **The break-up** — a respectful last call. "If I don't hear from you, I'll stop emailing, no hard feelings." A deadline, a single re-opt-in action, and a clean goodbye. This email reliably outperforms every other one in the sequence, because nothing reactivates a dormant subscriber like the genuine prospect of losing access.

A reactivation sequence built from discounts is a fire sale. A reactivation sequence built from this arc is a conversation that happens to end in an offer. Keep the order. The emotional logic only works if attention is rebuilt before the ask.

### Step 1: Gather the inputs

Before writing a single line, find out who is sending this and to whom. Ask the user for what you need, in small batches, and lean on anything already in the workspace (a Voice DNA file, a brand brief, past emails). Cover:

- **Who they are and what they sell** — the brand, the core offer, the audience. One or two sentences is fine.
- **Why these people originally subscribed** — the lead magnet, the promise, the topic. This is the thread you pull on to remind them why they cared.
- **The carrot for email 5** — what real reason can they give people to re-engage? A new offer, an event, a free resource, early access, a genuine discount. If they don't have one, the value give-back in email 2 can carry more weight and email 5 can lean on a flagship piece of content instead.
- **Constraints** — anything off-limits. No discounting? A specific sender name and reply-to? A product launch the sequence should lead into? Legal/compliance lines they must or must not use?
- **The send-from details** — the human name the emails come from, and whether replies should go to a monitored inbox (the check-in and the direct question both invite replies, so this matters).

### Step 2: Lock the voice

These emails have to sound like the user, not like marketing. If a Voice DNA document or a folder of their real emails exists in the workspace, read it and write from it. If not, ask for three or four of their actual emails or posts and pull the patterns yourself: how they open, sentence length, whether they swear or use emojis, the words they'd never use. Reactivation emails live or die on feeling personal, so a generic voice quietly kills the whole sequence. When in doubt, write plainer and warmer rather than slicker.

Use AskUserQuestion to settle anything the samples can't, especially:

- **Tone for the break-up email** — warm and self-deprecating, or crisp and matter-of-fact? Both work; they should pick.
- **Reply vs click** — do they want the early emails to drive one-line replies (great for deliverability and signal) or clicks to a page? Default to replies for emails 1 and 4 unless they say otherwise.

### Step 3: Write the six emails

Draft the full sequence in the structure above. Hold to these rules, because they are what separate a sequence that reactivates from one that gets archived:

- **One job per email.** If an email tries to check in *and* pitch *and* round up, it does none of them. Protect the arc.
- **No guilt.** "We noticed you've been ignoring us" makes people unsubscribe. Take the warm, on-their-side angle every time. The subscriber didn't do anything wrong.
- **Subjects earn the open on their own.** Write three per email, lead with the strongest, and make at least one of them curiosity-led and one plainly direct. Avoid spammy punctuation and false "RE:" tricks.
- **One CTA per email.** A reactivation email with five links splits the signal and tanks the click. Pick the single action that matters and remove the rest.
- **Keep them short.** Cold subscribers don't read long. The check-in and the direct question can be a few lines. Earn the longer email only where the content justifies it.
- **Make the break-up real.** It only works if the deadline and the consequence are genuine. The re-opt-in action must be a single, frictionless click or reply.
- **Write in their voice, anchored to evidence.** Mirror the patterns from their samples or Voice DNA, not a generic "brand-friendly" register.

### Step 4: Add the sending playbook

At the top of the file, write a short, practical note so the user can actually run this, not just admire it:

- **Who to send to** — the unengaged segment only (e.g. no opens/clicks in 90–365 days), never the whole list. Spell out how to build that segment in general terms.
- **Cadence** — space the six emails over roughly two to three weeks (a sensible default: Day 0, 3, 6, 10, 14, 18), adjusting to their normal sending rhythm.
- **Watch the signal** — anyone who opens, clicks, or replies moves back to the main list and drops out of the sequence; don't keep hitting re-engaged people with the break-up.
- **The sunset step** — after the break-up email, suppress or remove the remaining non-openers. This is the point of the exercise: a smaller, genuinely engaged list lands in more inboxes than a big dead one.
- **Deliverability caution** — if their list is large and very cold, send in smaller batches and warm up gradually, so a wave of bounces and spam complaints doesn't damage their sender reputation.

### Step 5: Deliver and offer the next step

Save the file as `[Brand] Reactivation Sequence.md` and present it. Offer to tune anything: rework the subject lines, harden or soften the break-up, adapt the sequence to their specific email platform, or fold in a Voice DNA file if they have one and didn't mention it.

### A note on judgement

Reactivation is partly a deliverability decision, not just a copy decision. If the user's real problem is that their whole list is cold and hurting their inbox placement, the most valuable outcome might be a smaller, cleaner list rather than a flood of re-opens, so frame success that way and don't over-promise that everyone comes back. Most won't, and that is the sequence working as intended.

## Hand-off

Deliver `[Brand] Reactivation Sequence.md` with the sending playbook at the top and the six emails below it. Tell the user exactly what to do next: build the unengaged segment, load the six emails on the suggested cadence, and plan to suppress the non-openers after the final email. Offer to adapt the copy for their specific platform or to write a short follow-up "welcome back" email for the people who re-engage.
