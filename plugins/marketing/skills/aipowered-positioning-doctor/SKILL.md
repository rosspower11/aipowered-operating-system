# Positioning Doctor

A skill for diagnosing weak positioning fast and returning a one-line statement a team can put on the homepage by tomorrow. Use this when a product describes itself as "the platform for [vague]" and nobody can repeat the pitch back, or when the homepage hero has been rewritten four times and nothing is landing.

## When to use this skill

- The homepage hero has changed three times in six months and conversion is flat
- Sales calls open with "so what does your product actually do?"
- Competitors are getting credit for moves the team made first
- A funding round, launch, or relaunch is forcing a positioning reset

## When NOT to use this skill

- For full messaging hierarchies (positioning is the headline; messaging is the body — different brief)
- For product naming exercises
- When the team hasn't talked to 10 customers in the last 90 days — go do that first, then come back

## How to run

Work in four steps. The whole diagnosis takes 30 minutes if the inputs exist.

### Step 1 — Diagnose the failure mode

Read the current homepage, the about page, and three recent sales decks. Tag the failure mode — usually one of five:

1. **Category trap** — pitched as "the platform for X" where X is so broad nobody knows what it is
2. **Feature soup** — pitched by feature list, not by what it replaces
3. **Hero of the wrong story** — pitched at the wrong buyer (champion's pain, not economic buyer's pain)
4. **Same-as-everyone** — the words on the homepage could appear on three competitors' homepages
5. **Inside-out** — pitched by what the team is proud of, not what the customer wants to be true

Name the failure mode out loud. "Your positioning has feature soup." Vagueness here makes the rest weak.

### Step 2 — Pull the evidence

Ask for three inputs, all at once:

- **Five recent customer quotes** — what they said the product replaced, in their words
- **Three lost-deal reasons** — why prospects chose someone else (or nothing)
- **Top three competitor homepages** — to find the words that are taken

If any input is missing, the diagnosis is a guess. Stop and get the data.

### Step 3 — Build the one-liner

Use this template, no deviation:

> [Product] is the [category] for [specific audience] who [specific situation], replacing [named alternative].

Worked example:

> Linear is the issue tracker for software teams who ship weekly, replacing Jira.

Each slot has a rule:

- **Category** — the simplest existing word the customer already uses. Don't invent categories.
- **Specific audience** — role + context, not just role. "Software teams who ship weekly" beats "engineers."
- **Specific situation** — when the pain shows up. The trigger, not the persona.
- **Named alternative** — a competitor, a spreadsheet, a manual process, or "doing nothing." Naming it forces clarity.

Write three variants. Pick the one you'd say first if a friend asked at dinner.

### Step 4 — Pressure-test against three readers

Show the one-liner to three people who don't know the product. Ask each: "What does it do? Who's it for? What does it replace?" If two of three can answer all three correctly, ship it. If not, the slots are still wrong — usually the audience or the alternative — and you go again.

## Hand-off

Deliver the diagnosis (named failure mode), the three one-liner variants, the recommended pick with a one-line reason, and a homepage hero rewrite using the chosen one-liner. Offer to follow up with the supporting messaging hierarchy once the one-liner has run live for two weeks. Don't keep wordsmithing in isolation — positioning is tested in the market, not in the doc.
