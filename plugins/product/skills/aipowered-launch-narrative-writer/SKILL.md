# Launch Narrative Writer

A skill for writing the narrative doc that decides whether a launch is real. Use this when a feature is being scoped that will need a website page, a Twitter thread, and a sales pitch — and you want to know now whether any of it sounds compelling. Working Backwards: if you can't write the press release, you don't have a launch. The output is an internal doc, never a published one.

## When to use this skill

- A feature is being considered and leadership wants to test whether it's worth building
- A launch is six to twelve weeks out and the positioning is still vague
- A team is building features but the marketing team can't describe the product in a sentence
- A founder is deciding which of three bets to lead with next quarter

## When NOT to use this skill

- For minor feature ships that don't deserve a launch moment
- For the actual external press release — this is the *internal* artefact, written before the work
- When the feature is purely internal-facing — write a memo, not a narrative

## How to run

### Phase 1 — The press release (1 page max)

Write it as if it's published the day of launch. Use this exact structure:

1. **Headline** — what the customer can do now that they couldn't before. Not a feature name, an outcome.
2. **Sub-headline** — who it's for, in one line.
3. **Opening paragraph** — city, date, the news.
4. **Problem paragraph** — the pain a real customer felt yesterday. Specific, named, with friction.
5. **Solution paragraph** — how this fixes it. Mechanism, not adjectives.
6. **Customer quote** — invented, but must sound like something a real customer would say. If it sounds like marketing, rewrite it.
7. **Internal quote** — from the CEO or product lead, naming the bet being made.
8. **Availability** — when, where, how much.

If the headline isn't compelling, stop. Don't fix the doc — fix the product idea.

### Phase 2 — The FAQ

Two halves. **External FAQ**: the five questions a customer or journalist will ask in the first hour — pricing, comparison to existing tools, migration, security, what's next. **Internal FAQ**: the ten questions an investor, board member, or sceptical engineer will ask — why now, why us, why not the obvious cheaper version, what's the wedge, what breaks at scale, what's the moat.

### Phase 3 — The strongest objection

One section, one page. Write the most damaging argument *against* the launch, in the voice of a thoughtful critic. Then either rebut it with evidence or concede that it's a known cost. Launches that ship without this section get torn apart in their first leadership review.

### Phase 4 — Pricing and metric

One paragraph each. If the launch has no defined success metric, the launch is theatre. Pick a number, attach a date.

### Style

- The press release reads like a press release a customer would forward, not an internal status update.
- No invented statistics. Use ranges or omit.
- No comparisons to specific competitors by name in the press release; use them in the FAQ.
- Customer quotes are short, ungrammatical-on-purpose, and sound like a human.

## Hand-off

Share the doc with product, design, eng, and GTM at the same time, with a 48-hour comment window. The narrative is approved when those four functions all sign off on the press release headline. If any one of them flinches, the launch isn't ready — fix the product, not the doc.
